## Executive Summary (≤200 words)

Between 460 and 430 BCE Athens sat at the peak of its self-confidence: the First Peloponnesian War ended in the Thirty Years' Peace of 446/445 BCE; the Delian League's treasury was moved from Delos to Athens in 454 BCE; Pericles became the unrivalled strategos and the driving force behind the Acropolis building programme (Parthenon 447–432, Propylaea begun c. 437 BCE, Erechtheion commissioned in this period but built 421–406 BCE). Citizenship was restricted by his law of 451 BCE. The city drew perhaps 30,000–60,000 adult male citizens, with metics and slaves bringing the Attic total to several hundred thousand; Piraeus, fed by Laurion silver and the trireme fleet, was the commercial lifeline. Religious life revolved around the Great Panathenaia and the City Dionysia, and the Stoa Poikile still displayed Marathon-era paintings. The period closes in 430 BCE with the arrival of the Plague via Piraeus and the death of Pericles in 429 BCE.

## 1. Political Frame

- **First Peloponnesian War (460–445 BCE)**
  Summary: A series of conflicts between the Delian League led by Athens (with Argos) and the Peloponnesian League led by Sparta (with Thebes). Athens lost Tanagra (457 BCE) but won Oenophyta and consolidated its position; reverses in Egypt (454 BCE) led to a five-year truce; Boeotia revolted after Coronea (446 BCE). The war ended in the Thirty Years' Peace.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Peloponnesian_War | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_Peace

- **Thirty Years' Peace (446/445 BCE)**
  Summary: Treaty between Athens and Sparta ending the First Peloponnesian War. Athens gave up Peloponnesian possessions (Megarian ports, Troezen, Achaea); Sparta recognised both leagues; neutral poleis could join either side. The treaty lasted only fifteen years before the Second Peloponnesian War.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_Peace

- **Long Walls of Athens (built 462–458 BCE; Middle Wall 457–444 BCE)**
  Summary: Two (later three) walls connecting Athens to Piraeus and Phalerum, ~6 km long. The "Middle Wall" was built under Pericles between 457 and 444 BCE. They were the city's strategic lifeline — guaranteeing seaborne resupply against Spartan land power.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Walls

- **Rise of Pericles (c. 495 – 429 BCE; dominant c. 461–429 BCE; strategos from c. 443 BCE)**
  Summary: Pericles, son of Xanthippus and Agariste of the Alcmaeonidai, dominated Athenian politics from c. 461 BCE and was re-elected annually to the generalship (strategos) after the ostracism of Thucydides son of Melesias in 442 BCE (the Wikipedia article gives 442; the brief specifies 443 — see Caveats). He held the rank of strategos and was acclaimed by Thucydides the historian as "the first citizen of Athens."
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategos

- **Delian League and the move of the treasury to Athens (454 BCE)**
  Summary: Founded 478 BC under Athenian hegemony; the Hellenotamiai (ten treasurers, one per tribe) were appointed in 477 BCE to receive allied contributions at Delos. In 454/453 BCE, on the proposal of the Samians, the treasury was transferred to Athens on the Acropolis — a symbolic step that converted the alliance into an Athenian empire.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenotamiae

- **Pericles' citizenship law (451 BCE)**
  Summary: A law restricting Athenian citizenship to children of two Athenian parents (was previously only paternal). Not retrospective, but applied at subsequent grain distributions (445 BCE). Metic legal status developed in the same decade.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metic | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy

- **Strategikon — the board of Ten Strategoi**
  Summary: From Cleisthenes' reforms (501 BCE), Athens elected ten strategoi annually, one per tribe, equal in status. They commanded campaigns by majority vote, rotating daily presidency. The strict one-per-tribe rule lasted until c. 440 BCE.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategos

## 2. Building Programme

- **Parthenon (447–432 BCE)**
  Summary: Temple of Athena Parthenos on the Acropolis, designed by Iktinos and Kallikrates with Phidias as overall sculptural director. Built of Pentelic marble; stylobate 69.5 × 30.9 m. Funded from the Delian League reserves (phoros). Frieze, metopes and pediments by Phidias' team. Completed 432 BCE.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon

- **Propylaea (begun c. 437 BCE)**
  Summary: Monumental gateway to the Acropolis commissioned by Pericles as part of the rebuilding programme after the Persian destruction. Designed by Mnesicles. Construction was suspended by the Peloponnesian War. Built wide enough for chariots. Unique in Greek architecture for using iron bars and marble girders for the roof beams.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylon

- **Erechtheion (commissioned in this period; constructed 421–406 BCE)**
  Summary: Ionic temple on the north side of the Acropolis, dedicated to Athena Polias and Poseidon-Erechtheus, with the famous Caryatid porch. Architect possibly Mnesicles. Construction began in 421 BCE — well after our period — but the commission belongs to the Periclean programme and is therefore still relevant as a building *imagined*. (Listed in Caveats.)
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheion

- **Ostracism of Thucydides son of Melesias (443 / 442 BCE)**
  Summary: Thucydides (not the historian) led the conservative opposition to Pericles, criticising the building programme as profligate. Pericles' counter-offer to repay all expenses from his own property carried the day. Thucydides was ostracised for ten years (Wikipedia gives 442 BCE; some sources cite 443 BCE — see Caveats). His exile left Pericles "unchallenged ruler of the Athenian political arena."
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles

- **Phidias / Pheidias and his team (c. 480 – c. 430 BCE)**
  Summary: Athenian sculptor, painter and architect; created the chryselephantine Athena Parthenos inside the Parthenon and the colossal bronze Athena Promachos that stood between the Parthenon and the Propylaea. Friends with Pericles; enemies of Pericles attacked him through Phidias, alleging theft of gold intended for the Athena statue. Iktinos and Kallikrates designed the Parthenon; Mnesicles the Propylaea.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidias | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon

- **Athena Promachos (c. 450 BCE)**
  Summary: Colossal bronze Athena by Phidias, towering between the Parthenon and the Propylaea; visible from the sea below Piraeus — a landmark for mariners and a statement of power.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidias

## 3. Population and Density

- **Adult male citizens of Athens (c. 450–431 BCE)**
  Summary: Consensus range 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens at the start of the Peloponnesian War; one estimate puts the mid-5th-century peak as high as 60,000 before the war and plague cut it down. 30,000 adult male voters is the most-cited figure.
  Evidence: plausible (modern estimate from indirect ancient data)
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy

- **Total population of Attica**
  Summary: With women, children, metics (~25,000 adult male metics at the start of the war) and slaves, the total Attic population in the mid-5th century is estimated at ~250,000–300,000, possibly higher. 150,000–250,000 is a conservative working range.
  Evidence: plausible
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metic

- **Piraeus population**
  Summary: Piraeus was Athens' commercial port and roughly half the size of the city in population during the 5th century BCE. Modern Piraeus municipality is ~168,000 (2021), but the classical port and its emporion were a fraction of that.
  Evidence: plausible
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus

- **The Kerameikos (potters' quarter, NW Athens)**
  Summary: Area NW of the Acropolis on both sides of the Dipylon Gate, on the Eridanos river. Inner Kerameikos was the potters' quarter; Outer Kerameikos was the public cemetery with the Dēmosion Sēma (where Pericles delivered his Funeral Oration in 431 BCE) and the start of the Sacred Way to Eleusis.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos

- **The Ancient Agora as civic centre**
  Summary: Located NW of the Acropolis, bounded south by the Areopagus, west by Agoraios Kolonos ("Market Hill"). The civic, commercial and political centre — surrounded by stoas, fountains, lawcourts, altars, the Stoa Poikile, the Royal Stoa, the Temple of Hephaestus, and later the Stoa of Zeus Eleutherios.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens

## 4. Economy

- **Silver from Laurion (state-owned mines, SE Attica)**
  Summary: The mines of Laurion (Lavrio) were the chief source of Athenian state revenue. Systematic exploitation began in the 6th c. BCE under Peisistratus; by the 5th c. they produced the silver that funded the trireme fleet and, after 454 BCE, the Acropolis building programme. Mines were leased to contractors (the same model of *poroi*).
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavrio

- **Trade via Piraeus and the emporion**
  Summary: Piraeus was Athens' commercial port, with its Hippodamean grid, ship sheds (neōria), and the emporion (market-of-foreigners). Grain came from the Hellespont and Black Sea; olive oil, wine, pottery, metalwork were exports. The Long Walls linked Piraeus to Athens.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Walls

- **Hellenotamiai (treasurers of the Greeks)**
  Summary: Board of ten treasurers, one per tribe, appointed from 477 BCE to receive allied contributions to the Delian League. Originally sat at Delos; after the treasury was transferred to Athens in 454/453 BCE they continued in office. From 453 BCE they paid the *aparchai* (First Fruits) to Athena and disbursed funds for war and construction.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenotamiae

- **Public slaves — the "Scythian archers" (skythes)**
  Summary: 300 (sometimes cited up to 1,200) public slaves in 5th-century Athens, named after their Scythian dress, used as a quasi-police force acting for the *Eleven* magistrates. Lived in tents or barracks in the Agora and (later) on the Areopagus. Represented in Attic vase painting.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythian_archers

- **Public festivals as state expenditure — Great Panathenaia & City Dionysia**
  Summary: Both festivals required massive public expenditure — sacrifices (hecatombs), processions, athletic and dramatic prizes. The *choregoi* (wealthy citizens) funded the dramatic choruses; the state funded the games. The Panathenaic Games ran every four years (the "Great Panathenaia") from 566 BCE onward; the City Dionysia featured tragedies and, from 487 BCE, comedies.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Games | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia

- **Theoric fund (later, 4th century)**
  Summary: A later (4th-century) institution for distributing surplus to citizens for festival attendance — *out of scope* for the 450–430 window; included here only so future agents don't accidentally introduce a 4th-century institution into the 5th-century scene.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenotamiae

## 5. Religion and Festivals

- **The Panathenaic procession**
  Summary: Annual (Lesser) and quadrennial (Great) procession honouring Athena. Route: gathered at the Pompeion near the Dipylon (later Roman-period Pompeion built c. 400 BCE, but the area between the Dipylon and the Sacred Gate is much older), through the Agora along the Panathenaic Way, up to the Eleusinion (north slope of Acropolis), and finally to the Erechtheion on the Acropolis. The peplos for Athena was prepared at the Pompeion, mounted on a ship-cart.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeion | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Games | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusinion

- **The City Dionysia**
  Summary: Major Athenian festival of Dionysus Eleuthereus held in the Theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis. Featured the pompe (procession with phalloi, kanephoroi, etc.), choregic competitions (dithyrambs, tragedies, comedies). The festival was the second most important after the Panathenaia.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Dionysus

- **The Theatre of Dionysus**
  Summary: Built on the south slope of the Acropolis as part of the sanctuary of Dionysus Eleuthereus. First orchestra terrace c. mid-to-late 6th c. BCE; the Periclean phase reorganised the theatre. The 4th-century Lycurgan expansion reached a capacity of up to 25,000.
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Dionysus

- **The Eleusinian Mysteries and the Eleusinion**
  Summary: Annual initiations of Demeter and Persephone. The Athens-side starting point was the Eleusinion (City Eleusinion) on the lower north slope of the Acropolis, just south of the Agora. From there the procession followed the Sacred Way (Hiera Hodos) west to Eleusis. Mysteries were open to freeborn Greek-speakers, women and men, slaves (with permission).
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusinian_Mysteries | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusinion

- **Sacrificial calendar — the rhythm of sacred time**
  Summary: Religious observance structured the month: *noumēnia* (new moon) and *dekatē* and other named days; sacrifices and festivals densely populated the calendar. The Dipolieia (Zeus Polieus festival, with the Bouphonia ox-sacrifice), Thesmophoria (Demeter, women-only), Skira, Pyanepsia, and many more.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens

## 6. Military

- **The hoplite phalanx**
  Summary: The citizen-army was a phalanx of heavy infantry (hoplites) from the *zeugitai* and higher census classes, fighting in close order with the round hoplon shield, thrusting spear, helmet and body armour. Each citizen provided his own panoply.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategos

- **The Athenian trireme fleet at Piraeus**
  Summary: Athens' main strategic asset. ~300+ triremes by 431 BCE, each rowed by 170 oarsmen (paid citizens or metics) and commanded by a trierarch (liturgy funded by wealthy citizens). Berthed in ship-sheds (neōria) at Piraeus and Zea. The fleet was the strategic counter to Sparta.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Walls

- **Athenian cavalry — the Hipparchoi**
  Summary: The Athenian cavalry (*hippeis*) was a small elite force (~1,000 in the 5th c. BCE) drawn from the wealthier classes, organised into ten *hipparchiai* under two *hipparchoi*. They mustered in the Agora and at cavalry parade grounds. They appear at the Hipparch monument in the Agora (the "Hipparch stoa" is best read as a reference to the cavalry officers' monument in/near the Agora).
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus_(cavalry_officer)

- **The Persian Wars memory — Marathon memorial, trophies**
  Summary: Marathon (490 BCE) was the formative memory of 5th-c. Athens. The Stoa Poikile ("Painted Stoa") in the Agora was built in the 460s BCE and housed paintings of Marathon (by Polygnotus and others). Persian-War trophies stood at Olympia and Delphi (the Serpent Column / "Triple Trident" survives).
  Evidence: archaeological + documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoa_Poikile

- **Persian Wars inscriptions on the Acropolis**
  Summary: The Persian-Helmet Votive, the Serpent Column (originally at Delphi), and dedications on the Acropolis kept the Persian-War memory vivid during the Periclean period. Many such dedications were melted down for coin in later crises.
  Evidence: archaeological
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Walls

## 7. Visual / Soundscape Notes

- **The Agora — open market, kapeleia, keryx**
  Summary: The Agora was noisy, crowded, and odorous. Permanent stalls (*kapeleia*) sold food, wine, pottery, metalwork; bronze-workers (*chalkeus*) hammered; the public herald (*keryx*) made announcements. The Stoa Poikile was a shaded walking gallery hung with paintings; the Royal Stoa held the eponymous archon. Criers, flutes, and the chanting of litigants added layers of sound.
  Evidence: plausible (literary + archaeological)
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoa_Poikile

- **The smell of the Kerameikos — pottery kilns**
  Summary: Inner Kerameikos was dominated by potters' workshops with permanent kilns firing figured pottery (Panathenaic prize amphorae, black- and red-figure wares), bronze foundries, and the clay mud of the Eridanos. The air was heavy with charcoal smoke, burnt clay, and the smell of slip.
  Evidence: archaeological
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos

- **The Piraeus — pitch, pine tar, ship-sheds**
  Summary: Piraeus' neōria (ship-sheds) leaked the smell of pitch (from the caulking of triremes) and pine tar (used as a preservative and as the resinous smell of new and salt-aged timber). The slipways and the *holkos* (haul-up ways) creaked under triremes being hauled out for maintenance; carpenters hammered; oars clattered. Sea-salt and fish-sauce (*garos*) hung over the emporion.
  Evidence: plausible
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus

- **Night-time lighting**
  Summary: Athens had no public street lighting. After dark, the city was lit by torches, oil lamps (*lychnoi*) at windows, hearths inside houses, and moon- and starlight at festivals (the Great Panathenaia procession moved through the night with torches; the Panathenaic torch-race was a sacred relay). Inside the theatre, torches and lanterns illuminated the action; the moon was important for evening sacrifices (e.g. the *pompa* of the Oschophoria).
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Games

## 8. Disease / Fragility

- **The Plague of Athens (430–426 BCE)**
  Summary: An epidemic that entered Athens through Piraeus in the second year of the Peloponnesian War (430 BCE), killing an estimated 75,000–100,000 people — perhaps 25% of the population. Pericles himself died of it in 429 BCE. Returns came in 429 and 427/426 BCE. Population took ~15 years to recover (Thucydides). Pathogen unidentified — typhoid is the leading hypothesis.
  Evidence: documented (Thuc. 2.47–54)
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens | https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Thuc.+2.34

- **Lead-in to the Plague — overcrowding**
  Summary: Pericles' strategy in 431 BCE of evacuating the Attic countryside into the urban walls of Athens and the connected city–Piraeus corridor produced massive overcrowding, sanitation collapse, and the conditions in which the disease spread.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens

- **The boundary of the period (430 BCE)**
  Summary: The 450–430 window stops *at the moment* the Plague breaks out. The period ends with the city still standing, the Acropolis still gleaming with the new Phidian marbles, Pericles still in command. The death of Pericles (autumn 429 BCE) and the plague's recurrences belong to the next period (out of scope, but flagged for narrative continuity).
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles

## 9. End of Period (430–429 BCE)

- **Outbreak of the Plague (430 BCE)**
  Summary: Disease arrives in Piraeus; spreads through the crowded city. Thucydides (who survived) describes the symptoms in detail.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens

- **Death of Pericles (autumn 429 BCE)**
  Summary: Pericles, strategos and "first citizen of Athens," died of the Plague in 429 BCE, along with his sister and most of his immediate family. His death ended the "Age of Pericles."
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles

- **Cleon (context only — 422 BCE death at Amphipolis)**
  Summary: *Out of scope.* Mentioned only so that narrative copy can identify the post-Periclean radical democrat who took over Pericles' political role. His death at Amphipolis in 422 BCE belongs to the next period.
  Evidence: documented
  SOURCES: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Peloponnesian_War

- **Hellenistic Athens** (after 322 BCE / Lamian War / Macedonian control).
- **Roman Athens** (after 86 BCE sack by Sulla, Roman Agora, Hadrian's arch, Herulian sack 267 CE).
- **Byzantine Athens** (after 529 CE Justinian closes the philosophical schools).
- **Ottoman Athens** (1456–1822 CE).
- **The "theoric fund" / fourth-century festival subsidies** — too late.
- **The Erechtheion's actual construction** — commissioned in this period but built 421–406 BCE.
- **Cleon's death at Amphipolis (422 BCE)** — flagged for context only.
- **First Sacred War (595 BCE)** — too early; outside the period.

## Caveats

- **Population numbers vary widely.** The consensus range is 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens at the start of the Peloponnesian War; some estimates push the mid-5th-century peak to 60,000; total Attic population estimates range from 150,000 to 300,000+ depending on the scholar. Use the range, never a single number.
- **Ostracism of Thucydides son of Melesias: 443 vs 442 BCE.** The Wikipedia "Pericles" article gives 442 BCE. Some secondary sources (e.g. The Cambridge Ancient History) give 443 BCE. Both are within accepted error margins. Recommend 443 BCE for the narrative year.
- **Pericles' citizenship law: 451 BCE date is robust**, but its enforcement, scope, and whether it was retrospective are still debated. Wikipedia: "Although the legislation was not retrospective, five years later… many 'illegitimate' citizens were removed from the registers" (445 BCE).
- **Treasury move date: 454 vs 453 BCE.** Wikipedia ("Delian League") gives 454 BCE; "Hellenotamiae" gives 454/453 BCE. Both are attested in the ancient sources. Use "c. 454 BCE" for narrative copy.
- **Plague dates: 430–426 BCE.** Plague breaks the period; we do not show the symptoms but we note the lead-in (430 BCE arrival via Piraeus, overcrowding) and the boundary.
- **The "Hipparch stoa" in the Agora.** Sources mention a Hipparch monument / cavalry officers' headquarters in/near the Agora; some references to a "Stoa of the Hipparchs" are post-Periclean or confused with other stoas. Best to speak of "cavalry officers' monument" rather than commit to a "Hipparch stoa."
- **Pompeion building (the marble structure).** The well-known marble Pompeion dates to c. 400 BCE (4th century). The Panathenaic procession assembling in the Kerameikos between the Dipylon and the Sacred Gate is older; for 450–430 BCE we should refer to the *area*, not the marble building.
- **Erechtheion.** Commissioned as part of the Periclean programme, but construction began in 421 BCE — *after* our window. Show only as a site for the Panathenaic procession destination (the *oikos* of Athena Polias existed before the Erechtheion).
- **Stoa Poikile paintings.** Original paintings lost; documented in literary sources (Pausanias, Pliny, Aeschines). The Battle of Marathon painting was the key exhibit.
- **Spartan cavalry / Panathenaic "torch races"** — included here as a plausible/traditional element; the documentary record is fragmentary.
- **British Museum and ASCSA primary pages were inaccessible** during this research run (Cloudflare / 60x errors); ToposText is a stable substitute. The British Museum Parthenon / sculpture pages were not retrieved; rely on Wikipedia for the Acropolis sculptures and on Perseus for ancient-literature quotations.
